Dear colleagues, dear students.

Last week, I was meandering through the hair and beauty aisle in my favourite shop and I noticed something different about my usual shampoo. It was marked as "refillable". I am sure you are all familiar with the concept: instead of buying a new bottle of shampoo every time you finish one, you just buy a top-up pack and squeeze the contents into the bottle you already have. 

You may have noticed that there has been a rise in the number of beauty brands launching these refillable products. Quite frankly, it feels like hardly a day goes by without another beauty company announcing that something is "now refillable!". Beauty companies all seem to follow the same strategy: cheaper brands like Dove, as well as more expensive brands, luxury players like Chanel, they all seem to have jumped on the refillable bandwagon.

When it comes to marketing those refills, some brands will emphasize that you save money, because the refills typically cost less than the original products. But pretty much all brands will point to the fact that using refills generates less waste than buying the primary packaging over and over again. Now, that brings me to the topic of my speech, that is to say, the problem of waste in the beauty industry.

Now, the main problem for the beauty industry in terms of waste is indeed packaging, as packaging accounts for 70 percent of the sector's waste. We're talking about over 120 billion units of plastic packaging waste each year. According to a Greenpeace report, only two types of plastic can be considered recyclable and unfortunately, they are the least used in packaging. 

Now, that all sounds very grim. The good news, however, is that the public is increasingly motivated to do something about plastic. In the UK, for instance, three-quarters of shoppers would be happy to pay more for brands with sustainable packaging. As a result, most businesses are rethinking their packaging and their overall environmental policies. Obviously, the cosmetics industry has to follow suit, or otherwise it would have an image problem. Hence the wave of refillable products, because, as I've mentioned before, using refills generates less waste than buying the primary packaging over and over again. So those refillable products look like an easy win for the planet — but looks can be deceiving. As it turns out, it is not that simple. 

Like recyclability, refillability is great in theory but it is not always great in practice. Just as certain materials are able to be recycled, certain types of packaging are able to be refilled, sure, but the question on both counts is: will they be? I'm afraid not.
First of all, a refill probably will not have the same appeal, it will not be as aesthetically pleasing as the original product. 

Second, consumers will have to accept the reality that some refills are clunky to use: I mean how are we supposed to handle those liquid-filled pouches that so easily spill during transfer. There is no point in manufacturing a durable, reusable vessel if the consumer uses it only once or twice and then says: "Forget it. Too much of a hassle."

And third, the main issue here, I think: do consumers actually want to use the same formulas over and over again? Probably not. Refills simply are not the right thing for every product. There's so much experimentation in beauty and not enough brand loyalty to justify the refill. Take refillable lipsticks. According to some estimates, you would need to refill them some 50 to 100 times before you've made a positive impact. It begs the question: how many beauty products have you repurchased more than 50 times? If I am honest, the answer for me is: zero.

However, the waste problem in the beauty industry is not just limited to packaging. There's also the problem of chemical waste. Many beauty products contain synthetic chemicals like parabens. These do not break down in landfills, they pollute nearby water, adding to overall pollution levels. 

And what about the real scandal in the beauty industry? A huge number of beauty products are destroyed each year by manufacturers and retailers without ever hitting the market. These unsold products are another huge cause of waste in the beauty industry. 

I want to give you some figures to really show you the extent of the problem. In the UK alone, 90 million beauty products are thrown away each year. That is the weight of 650 doubledecker buses. We are talking about 4,500 tonnes of moisturiser, 1,000 tonnes of mascara, and 4 Olympic-sized swimming pools full of perfume. 

And then I haven't even mentioned the use and waste of water in the industry. It is estimated that the cosmetics industry uses 78 billion litres of water annually to create products. After all, water is the most common ingredient in conventional cosmetics. 

In summary, sustainability in the beauty industry is a complex journey. "Refillable" may not be the answer to all problems, in fact, it may well be just another buzzword, but at least it raises awareness, which I suppose is key in bringing about true change.

Thank you for your attention.
