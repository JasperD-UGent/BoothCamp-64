If you know me, then you know that I work as a teacher and as a teacher, there's something I notice each and every year. It is the fact that some students always look exhausted. Now, just to be clear, I'm not talking about the party animals, nor am I talking about the ones who stay up all night to play video games or to binge-watch Netflix series. I am referring to bright, hardworking students who, unfortunately, are forced to juggle two or three jobs to cover their tuition and rent. It really pains me to see that they are too tired to focus. It breaks my heart to see that they turn down unpaid internships they really want because they cannot afford to work for free.

Things like that stay with you as a teacher. You see the potential, but you also see the financial burden holding students back. And sometimes I wonder: what if, what if these students had a guaranteed income each month? Not huge amounts, just enough for them to study without that constant financial pressure. Well, that "what if", that safety net, is what I would like to talk about today. I will be talking about the so-called Universal Basic Income or UBI. 

First, I will walk you through the definition of UBI, then I will discuss some experiments, and then I will cover the pros and cons. 

So, first things first. what is Universal Basic Income? Well, the idea is pretty simple: it is a type of payment. Now, that of course sounds very vague. I would say that there are 5 key characteristics.

First of all, this payment is periodic. In other words, payments happen not just once, but regularly, for example monthly. Secondly, it is a cash payment, so people can spend it as they see fit. Thirdly, this payment is unconditional. So there are no requirements you need to meet. You do not have to be employed, for instance, to receive the payment. The fourth characteristic is that it is universal. In other words, it is given to everyone in the population or in a defined group. And last but not least, it is individual: it is given to individual people, rather than households.

Now that you know what UBI is, let's move on to some experiments. We can indeed only look at experiments or pilot projects, because to my knowledge, UBI has not been fully implemented anywhere yet. No country has offered UBI to its entire population.

In Europe, Finland, Germany, Spain and the Netherlands ran limited basic income programs. In the case of Finland, participants received €560 a month for 2 years without any work requirements. 

In developing countries, there were trials in places like Brazil, Kenya, India and Namibia. 

Even though limited in scope, these experiments are valuable because they give real data: how people use the money, how it affects their overall health and happiness, etc. So I think they allow us some insights into pros and cons.

Let's start with some arguments in favour of UBI.

UBI can lift people out of poverty. Let me give you the example of Brazil. The poverty rate there fell to its lowest level in 40 years in just 6 months' time. This happened in 2020 after $110 (600 reais) per month was distributed to about 25% of the population as a relief program to counter the effects of the pandemic. 

You might be wondering how much money a developed country like the US would have to spend to end poverty. Well, according to an economist, a UBI set at $1,000 per adult per month and $300 per child per month would entirely eradicate poverty in the US.

The experiments also showed that UBI can improve physical and mental health by reducing stress, by allowing people to afford basic needs, such as medicine or healthy food. In Namibia, child malnutrition rates fell from 42% to 17% in just 6 months, as a result of a UBI programme.

Another advantage is that people have more freedom to change jobs or to invest in education. To take the example of Namibia again, as a result of UBI, school dropout rates fell from almost 40% in November of 2007 to 5% in June of 2008, and to almost 0% in November of 2008. That's because it became easier for parents to afford school fees, uniforms and supplies.

Ending poverty, improving health, reducing dropout rates. That all sounds like music to my ears. How could anyone be against it? Well, of course it is not all rainbows and sunshine. There are some drawbacks. 

UBI comes with a steep price tag. The cost is probably the biggest challenge, resulting in large public deficits or a higher tax burden. Earlier on I talked about a trial in Finland. If the entire population were to benefit, it would increase the government deficit by about 5 percent.
Some also say that if you pay people unconditionally, to do nothing ... they will do nothing, leading to a less effective economy.

Furthermore, because universal payments go to everyone, even the wealthy, some argue that money is wasted on people who do not need it. 

So, where does all this leave us? Universal Basic Income isn't a magic wand, and it won't solve every problem overnight. But it does force us to ask some uncomfortable, and important, questions: Do we want a society where people live one paycheck away from crisis? Or do we want a society where everyone has at least a basic level of security, no matter what? Whether or not we adopt it fully, UBI challenges us to rethink what fairness and social rights should look like in the 21st century. And that, I think, is a debate well worth having.
