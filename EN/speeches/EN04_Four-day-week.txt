Dear colleagues, dear students, I'd like to talk to you today about work-life balance, about an innovative idea to improve work-life balance. So close your eyes and picture this: What if … What if you could earn a full-time income while still having plenty of time left every week? In other words: you have plenty of time left to tackle the household chores AND you have plenty of time left to enjoy your hobbies as well. Dear colleagues, dear students, does that sound like your dream life? Well, I must say it certainly sounds like music to my ears. Yet, I imagine some of you are now thinking: if it sounds too good to be true, it probably is. 

However, the picture I'm painting here is actually a realistic one. Your dream life could really be within reach. That is to say, if you consider moving to Iceland. You see, Iceland experimented with a four-day workweek, which turned out to be an overwhelming success. So I'd like to tell you a bit more about the experiment. Let's look at the details of the trials, which took place over a three-year period.

First of all, you might be wondering who set up these trials and who participated in them. Well, it was a team effort really, they were run by the Reykjavík City Council in cooperation with the national government. And more than 2,500 workers participated. To put that figure into perspective: 2,500 workers, that's the equivalent of about 1% of Iceland's total working population. These workers came from a wide range of workplaces, including preschools, offices, social service providers, and hospitals.

So what changes were made to a typical workweek then? Exactly what changed for these 2,500 people? All of them moved from their usual 40-hour workweek to a 35 or 36-hour workweek. But, and here's the kicker: they were still paid the same as before. So they kept their original 40-hour-week salary, even though they were working 4 or 5 hours less a week than they had done before.

And here's the part you've been waiting for, because you're of course all dying to find out about the outcome. So what about the results of this experiment?
Let me first say something about the workers' productivity. Remember, they worked 4 to 5 hours less each week. And yet, their productivity didn't decline. It remained the same. In fact, believe it or not, the majority of workplaces even saw a rise in productivity.

Another outcome of the study has to do with wellbeing. Workers reported feeling less stressed, less exhausted, and less at risk of burnout. They said their health and their work-life balance had drastically improved thanks to the experiment. They also reported having more time to spend with their families, to enjoy their hobbies, and to complete household chores.

But that's not all. The experiment didn't just yield positive results in terms of productivity and well-being. Apparently, shorter working hours also led to significant cuts in carbon emissions. You might wonder exactly how a four-day workweek helps to do that. Well, it's quite simple, really. 

First of all, employees commute to their workplaces daily, contributing significantly to carbon emissions. By reducing the number of commuting days from 5 to 4, we can decrease the number of vehicles on the road, and decrease the consumption of fossil fuels. So this reduction in commuting days directly translates to lower carbon emissions. 

And there's even more. It goes without saying that office buildings are energy-intensive spaces. They need to be heated or cooled; lights must be kept on, and computers, printers, coffee machines, and other equipment must be powered. By reducing workdays, the need for heating, cooling, and lighting diminishes and fewer electronic devices require powering. This is a notable reduction in energy consumption, which leads to lower carbon emissions.

So, dear colleagues, dear students, I think it's safe to conclude that these trials in Iceland were overwhelmingly positive. They led unions to renegotiate working patterns, and now 86% of Iceland's workforce have either moved to shorter hours for the same pay already, or they will gain the right to do so shortly.

So, what about you? Are you convinced? But perhaps not quite willing to move to Iceland because you're a bit of a cold-o-phobe? Then I've got good news for you! Similar trials are now being run across the world, including in sunnier destinations. Spain, for example, is piloting a four-day workweek too. And so is New Zealand. Consumer goods giant Unilever is giving its staff in New Zealand a chance to cut their hours by 20% without hurting their pay.

In conclusion, the Icelandic shorter working week tells us it is possible to work less in modern times. I do hope that Belgium follows suit, because to be perfectly honest with you, I wouldn't mind having an extra day to exercise, to bake scones, to do a little water-colouring, and to spend more quality time with family and friends. So there you have it. Maybe we really can have our cake and eat it too.
