Dear students, dear colleagues.

The topic of today's speech is insects. Now, I imagine some of you may be getting slightly uncomfortable, as, after all, many people are scared of insects – and to be completely honest with you, I am not a huge fan of bugs myself – but don't worry, I promise I will not show you any pictures of creepy crawlies. I just want to introduce you to the so-called 'superhighways' that British conservationists are creating. These superhighways are highways, not for people, but for insects.

I can just about hear you thinking: Wait, what? Highways for bugs? If you are not familiar with the concept yet, then I certainly understand that that probably sounds like a harebrained idea for a bad horror film. So I suggest we start at the very beginning. It is common knowledge that traditional highways, much like other roads and railways, have largely facilitated human travel. Unfortunately, they have had the opposite effect for insects, because highways – and the housing developments, industrial sites, farms and transport infrastructure that come with them – have all contributed to the fragmentation of insect habitats. 

Let me give you a figure to corroborate that. Since the 1930s, England and Wales have lost 97 per cent of their wildflower meadows. To put that number into perspective, that is a land mass roughly 1.5 times the size of Wales. So, it's crystal clear that a massive amount of habitat has been lost, which means that many insects have been left high and dry, marooned on shrinking islands of biodiversity. 

To address this issue, an insect charity called Buglife set up a conservation project, which aims to join the dots between existing meadows, enabling insects to move freely between them. In other words, the initiative is creating a network of wildflower superhighways across the UK. Perhaps I should clarify that whenever I mention "superhighways", I mean "stepping stones" rather than continuous corridors of flowers, really. 

Buglife has been busy lately, busy as a bee, pardon the pun. It has spent the last decade mapping potential routes for these insect superhighways, which financially, has not exactly been a walk in the park. Let me explain why the project has cost the proverbial arm and leg.

Well, first of all, the charity needed to cough up a hefty sum to gain access to the land-use data needed to create the map. Obtaining those data set them back around £2,500 per county. And then in addition to that, of course you can't forget the working hours needed to collect the data and identify the best routes for insects, a task easier said than done. 

So this is an expensive conservation project and it may not be achieved completely, which might not necessarily be a problem, though. Apparently, if just 10 per cent of the proposed network comes to fruition, that could be enough to give the UK's ailing insects a much-needed boost. 

First of all, because it could help to prevent interbreeding, that is to say, breeding between different species, which happens when insects are isolated in fragmented habitats. 

Secondly, it could also offer essential routes out of habitats that become too hot due to the climate crisis. 

Unfortunately, however, this project will not address the other main cause of bug declines in the developed world, namely the widespread use of pesticides. But that is another can of worms and Rome was not built in a day either.

Anyway, how far down the road is Buglife with its superhighways? Pilot sections of this network have already been completed, near Cardiff and Norwich. While it is true that those efforts are just the tip of the iceberg, at least the wheels are in motion.

Anyone living along one of the proposed routes can get involved in the project. All they need to do is let their lawns grow, or even just create a small herb garden, which is basically like creating a "motorway service station for bees".

Dear students, dear colleagues, I hope this story has inspired you to create your own meadow for bees and to let your lawn grow. The only drawback is that your neighbours may call you lazy. After all, not everyone is buzzing with excitement when it comes to untended lawns. Explaining yourself might even make things worse. I suppose they will call you crazy if you say that you are operating a motorway service station for bees. 

But please do not let negative comments stop you from chipping in. Let us not forget: insects may be small, but these little creatures punch well above their weight. They pollinate our crops, enrich our soils, and keep ecosystems ticking like well-oiled machines. So while planting a few herbs or letting your lawn grow might seem like a drop in the ocean, every little bit helps. Together we can turn the tide. After all, many hands make light work.
