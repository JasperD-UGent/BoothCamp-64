Dear colleagues, dear students.

In this speech I would like to explore the topic of how to include people with a disability in the workplace. I am afraid that all too often "we", as in those of us without disabilities, just do not realise how many barriers and challenges disabled people face on a day-to-day basis. I say "we", but I can really only speak for myself. I daresay having a student in a wheelchair a couple of years ago was an unexpected eye-opener.

I distinctly remember taking our interpreting students on a guided study visit to the LEX building, an official EU building in Brussels. At lunch time, we all headed for the cafeteria. But the security gates to reach that cafeteria were too narrow for the wheelchair to pass through. Important note: the wheelchair in question belonged to the smallest types available on the market. Eventually one of the kitchen workers, who noticed our struggle and cared enough to actually intervene, escorted us through the kitchen, past hot pans and busy staff, to access a service lift to the cafeteria. All that fuss, just for a simple meal. 

Now, the building where this happened, the LEX building, is relatively new. Construction started in 2004 and was finished in 2006, so the building has been in use for about 20 years. It cost around 262 million euros. Given the building's steep price tag, accessibility should have been more than an afterthought, I reckon, especially as it houses 1,200 Council staff members including 800 translators. It has 7 meeting rooms, 2 of which are fully equipped with 29 interpreting booths. The building also houses the Council's archives and libraries, along with the aforementioned canteen, which seats 400 people. Surely, our student cannot have been the first person in a wheelchair to try and access the canteen? 

Needless to say, this was not the only wheelchair accessibility challenge. On another occasion, at the Court of Justice in Luxembourg, a portable ramp had to be brought in for our student to be able to reach the interpreting booths. Speaking of which, we also had to install a ramp to enter our interpreting booths here at university. 

And I have not even started on the daily grind: the logistical hassle of taking a train or bus to reach those buildings in the first place. If you are in a wheelchair, you cannot simply take the train whenever and wherever you want. You have to book an appointment in advance, so a dedicated staff member meets you at the right platform and brings in a ramp. Needless to say, that service is not available everywhere, 24/7, neither will public transport be fully accessible without any assistance in the near future.

To some, these incidents may sound like minor inconveniences, but these moments all add up over time. They clearly show that our institutions and infrastructures are still not perfectly designed for everyone, unfortunately.

Let me present you some figures to show the scale of the gap we still need to close. 

In the UK alone, 8.4 million adults report being disabled. Just to make sure that we are all on the same page, by "adults" I mean "working-age adults", so people aged 16 to 64. In that age bracket, 8.4 million people are disabled in the UK. Of those, 4.4 million currently have a job, giving us an employment rate of 52.3%. 

The employment rate of non-disabled people is much higher. The difference between the 2 is 28.8 percentage points, but that is of course an average number. It is actually even higher for men, but lower for women. And gender is not the only factor contributing to the divide; age is a relevant factor too. Higher age groups show a more pronounced employment gap between non-disabled and disabled people.

Fortunately, the latest government research shows some progress. The good news is that the majority of employers are open to creating a more supportive environment for disabled candidates and employees. 84% of employers believe disabled people make a valuable contribution to the workplace and 71% are willing to make adjustments. 

Some straightforward, practical changes can make a considerable difference to the working environment for a person with disabilities. 

First, there are of course obvious modifications to improve physical access to a building or office. Just cast your mind back to my introduction: widening doorways, adjusting entrances, and providing ramps can already make a world of difference for wheelchair users. 

Then there is also reasonable accommodation, modifications so employees can actually do their job once they have made it into their office: larger computer screens, safely accessible sockets, and assistive technology, such as screen readers and magnifiers.

Reasonable accommodation could also include flexible working hours or remote work options, allowing employees to avoid rush hour or attend necessary weekday doctors' appointments.

These accommodations tend to come at an extra cost for employers, but it is in fact a win-win. Removing barriers allows us to expand the talent pool, help employees feel valued, boost morale, and spark creativity and innovation by bringing more different perspectives to the table.

Thank you for your attention.
