In this presentation, I'll be talking about locusts. Now, when you think of locusts, the first thing that comes to mind might well be a biblical story. The one that Jewish people around the world retell every year to celebrate Passover. I'm talking about the Exodus from Egypt. That includes the ten plagues. You know, the plagues that God inflicted on the Ancient Egyptians: water turning to blood, frogs, lice, flies, livestock pestilence, boils, hail, darkness, the killing of firstborn children and indeed, locusts.

However, locust plagues aren't just a thing of the past. To this day, they're quite a common problem in many African countries. It's hard to deal with swarms of locusts. That's because their movements are quick and unpredictable. Swarms can travel up to 480 kilometres in one day. That means they easily migrate from one country to another. So it's challenging to contain them. There are plenty more challenges, but I'll briefly discuss 3 with you today.

The first one is a financial challenge. Fighting locusts is expensive. That's because it's not only the extermination as such that requires money. You also need to help the affected households to meet their immediate food needs. And then, later on, follow-up actions are necessary. You need to rehabilitate food production. You know, you need to get communities back on their feet. So any approach – whether it involves traditional pesticides or more advanced techniques – any approach requires massive funding. 

Secondly, there's also climate change. Climate change is a key driver of recent outbreaks. Unusual weather conditions generate strong cyclones and heavy rains in the Arabian Peninsula. These wet conditions trigger higher-than-normal vegetation growth. That, in turn, creates ideal conditions for locusts. 

A third major challenge in controlling locusts is political instability and terrorism. Some of the most affected countries are also some of the most unstable in East Africa. UN agencies have committed to fighting locusts. However, they rarely risk taking their personnel to countries like Somalia. That's because Somalia experiences frequent terrorist attacks from al-Shabab militants. So political stability and international cooperation are crucial in dealing with locusts. 

Now, let me tell you a bit more about the different ways of dealing with locust swarms.

Chemical pesticides are the most obvious solution. Pesticides tend to be the first line of defense. So far, Kenya alone has used 1,798,000 litres of chemical pesticides to deal with its latest locust plague. Spraying – effective though it may be – comes with drawbacks. The substances that are used can be harmful, both to the environment and to human health. I mean, these broad-spectrum pesticides are not only effective at killing locusts. Unfortunately, they also kill bees and other insects. Moreover, they leach into water systems and can damage human health. 

Another option is to use biological pesticides. Biological pesticides are based on fungi that infect and kill locusts. These fungus-based pesticides are thought to be harmful to a narrower range of species than conventional pesticides. So they pose less risk. However, some researchers have suggested that these biological pesticides could also harm other species. Moreover, they take longer to kill the pests than chemical pesticides. In other words, you risk greater crop damage.

Alternatives, then. Alternatives include the use of drones and electrified metal grids. Drones can be navigated to fly low enough to do surveillance. So there's no need for larger planes with human pilots. Electric grids can be dragged over fields to generate vibrations. The idea is to scare the locusts away and to shock those that it comes into contact with. 

Another solution is to catch as many locusts as possible, to use them as animal feed. This way, you can turn the threat into an asset. During the day, locusts keep flying around. Of course, that makes it impossible to catch them. So the trick is to harvest them at night. That's when they are sleeping on shrubs. Local farmers collect, crush and dry these locusts. They then grind them into a powder. They can sell that for 0.46 dollars a kilo. So farmers have an additional financial incentive to kill and collect locusts. 

And why only feed locusts to animals? For centuries, people have consumed bugs, everything from beetles to dragonflies, termites and caterpillars. In fact, insects still form a big part of everyday diets for two billion people around the world. The practice even has a name: entomophagy. Scientists say entomophagy could be a cost-effective solution for developing countries that are food insecure. After all, insects are rich in proteins. They're also high in fat and fibre. And they contain micronutrients, such as vitamins and minerals. 

You have to overcome the "ick factor", of course. But, apparently, bugs can be delicious. Mealworms taste like roasted nuts. Crickets have an aroma of popcorn. Locusts are similar to shrimp. So why not consider serving some at your next dinner party? Let me know how it goes, if you decide to give it a try!
