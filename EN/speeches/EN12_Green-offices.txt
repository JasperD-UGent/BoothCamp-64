Dear colleagues, dear students.

I remember when I was a student, I could concentrate for hours on end. I would get started on a task – say memorising some vocabulary lists – and by the time I would look up at the old clock, hours had passed, even though those hours felt like mere minutes. That is what researchers call a flow state: when you're so absorbed in what you're doing that you lose all sense of yourself, and time seems to fall away into complete, effortless focus. It is the most profound form of attention we know of.

I'm reluctant to admit that nowadays, I often genuinely struggle to concentrate. On some days, even 15 minutes of sustained focus feels unattainable. I probably do not need to point out that constant interruptions are a productivity killer. I'm sure you will all be aware that if you're interrupted, it takes no less than 23 minutes for you to regain the same state of focus. 

However, perhaps luckily for me, it seems I'm not the only one. According to researchers, there are many reasons why we cannot concentrate. These reasons range from the intrusive impact of technology (the constant high-pitched pings and dings on our phones), over higher stress levels, to deteriorating diets and rising pollution.

Now, I won't dive into the reasons why many of us find it increasingly harder to focus. Instead, I would like to explain how nature can help us restore our focus. So, I'll talk about 4 scientific experiments about nature and concentration.

A classic study in this field was conducted in 2007. Psychologists asked participants to perform a demanding task to test their attentional abilities. These participants heard several lists of numbers, numbers which varied from 3 to 9 digits in length. And then the participants were asked to repeat them in backward order. 

After that task, all of the participants went out for a 1-hour walk. Half of them went for a stroll in a leafy local arboretum, the other in a noisy, heavily congested city centre. Then all participants came back and repeated the attention task. A week later, they swapped circumstances: each person had to go on the walk not taken the previous week.

So what about the results? Well, task performance only improved considerably after the nature walk, not after the city walk. Moreover, the result didn't depend on whether participants took their walks during the idyllic summer or gloomy winter. No matter what time of year, the nature walk enhanced their attention more than the city walk.

Hearing these results might worry you, especially if you live in the city. But rest assured, you don't actually need to go out into nature to benefit from it. Exposure to the natural world through photos and videos apparently also restores our attention.

An experiment published in 2016 induced stress in participants. Afterwards, they watched a 6-minute video of neighbourhood streets that varied in their green views. At the low end, participants watched a video of homes on a street without any trees. At the high end, the video showed a neighbourhood with a lush canopy of trees. 

The participants who were exposed to the most views of nature showed a 60%-increase in their ability to recover from the stress compared with those who saw videos without any green spaces.

So if you don't have the time to go out, or if you don't have a green area near your home or your workplace, just watch some photos of nature on your computer. Virtual nature is, incredibly, still nature as far as the human mind is concerned. 

Or there is also the option of bringing the natural world inside your office, not through photos or videos but in the form of houseplants. British researchers found that indoor plants can improve concentration, productivity and well-being by 47%. They also discovered that plants can boost memory by up to 20%. 

Now, these effects probably come from a plant's oxygenating abilities. Since many of us spend several hours indoors, we're exposed to higher amounts of carbon dioxide. Higher levels of CO2 make it harder to concentrate, think clearly, and make informed decisions. The oxygen generated by indoor plants, however, helps balance the levels of carbon dioxide in our bodies. So office plants can give you a breath of fresh air right at your desk.

Now, the bulk of the research on the psychological benefits of nature focuses on visual exposure, like watching photos or videos of nature. However, our other senses can definitely play a part as well. In 2019, a study found that exposing people to natural sounds, such as subtle crickets chirping, improved performance on an attentional task.

And again, the good news is you can access those sounds even while you are in the office. You may have heard that Nature has recently become an official artist on all major streaming platforms. So if you listen to sounds like gentle ocean waves, wind, rainstorms, or melodic birdsong on Spotify, you are not just boosting your concentration. You are also funding conservation projects worldwide. That is because Nature's royalties will be collected and directed to biodiversity projects around the world. Now that is what I call a win-win situation.

Thank you for your attention.
