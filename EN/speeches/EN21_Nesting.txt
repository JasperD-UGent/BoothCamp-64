Dear colleagues, today I'd like to talk about divorce. I may not have any first-hand experience with divorce, but lately, the topic has been on my mind a bit more often than usual. That is because my best friend's marriage is on the rocks. Both she and her husband have brought up the idea of a divorce to me. And then the conversation quickly veered to 2 main questions. First of all: "What about the kids?" They clearly want to protect their children from the emotional turmoil of a breakup, as I suppose most parents do. And the second question, more of a financial issue: "What about the house?" Can one of them buy out the other one to keep the house or do they need to sell it?

And it's in that context, the context of kids and the family home during a divorce, that they taught me a new word, namely "birdnesting". Apparently, it's an increasingly popular concept and I'll tell you all about it in this speech. First, I'll walk you through the definition. Then we'll look at the effects of birdnesting – on children on the one hand and on parents on the other. And then we can hopefully answer the question whether or not this is a sustainable solution for the future.

Alright. Let's begin with the basics. I promised you a definition. So what exactly is birdnesting?

Birdnesting is a post-divorce arrangement. It's an arrangement where children remain living in the family home full-time. It's the parents who alternate. They take turns living in the family home. When a parent is "off-duty", when it's not their turn, not their week to take care of the children, they stay elsewhere. That "elsewhere" could be a rented space (a house or an apartment) or even a room with relatives, maybe their old bedroom at their own parents' place.

Okay, you now know what birdnesting is and how it works. Let's now move on to perhaps the most important question: Is birdnesting better for kids? 

Supporters say yes, because the arrangement creates more stability and offers children more emotional protection. It helps children avoid feeling like they're being passed back and forth like luggage. Instead, they feel that it's the parents who are adapting. Children stay in a safe little bubble, in their familiar environment. They maintain their school routine, they keep their bedrooms, and they continue to see their friends. This sense of continuity can help reduce the trauma of a divorce.

However, not all children experience it positively. Critics argue that birdnesting may confuse kids. It creates a confusing place where the reality of divorce is hidden rather than faced. In some cases, children might not fully understand whether their parents are truly separated or just taking a break. Also, by sheltering kids too much, we may be denying them the chance to build resilience. Facing change – like moving to a new home – can be challenging but it can also be character-building if handled supportively.

So, is birdnesting better for kids? Well, I think there isn't enough long-term research yet to prove whether or not it is better for children's mental health. So while birdnesting sounds great in theory, the evidence is still developing.

But of course, the children are only half of the equation here. Let's not forget about the parents. How does birdnesting affect them?

Many parents find birdnesting beneficial, at least in the short term, because it gives them time to adjust. Nesting can offer a transition period, giving parents breathing room to figure out their next steps. It can help them avoid rushed financial or emotional decisions in the immediate aftermath of a separation. It allows parents to prioritize their children's needs before jumping headfirst into solo living and all the challenges that brings.

For some, birdnesting may also make sense financially. Parents may be able to delay selling the family home and avoid some legal costs. However, whether it's really cost-effective or not I think will depend on their individual living arrangements during their weeks off, in the weeks where they don't have to look after the children. I mean, living with family will probably be a lot cheaper than renting or buying another property. Then you're essentially financing 2 places.

So the financial aspect can be a downside. And also from an emotional point of view, it's far from plain sailing. Birdnesting can be emotionally draining for adults. Many parents report feeling stuck. They can't really start rebuilding their own lives, because there is no clear break. Not to mention the logistical complications: shuffling between places and living out of bags can create stress.

So, dear colleagues, is birdnesting a model for the future? While it might look like a peaceful arrangement from the outside, the reality can be emotionally complex for everyone involved. So perhaps the best way to think about birdnesting is that it's an option, not a magic solution. It works well for some families, especially in the short term, but it's not a one-size-fits-all fix.

In the case of my friend, I secretly hope that a therapist can reconcile them so it doesn't come to birdnesting or, indeed, a divorce. If they can't see eye to eye now, they certainly won't be able to while rotating houses every week.

Thank you for your attention.
