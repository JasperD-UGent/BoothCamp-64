Ladies and gentlemen.

One of the biggest health issues in the world today is obesity among children. And when I say a big problem, I really do mean a "big" problem. Between 2021 and 2024, 19.7% of American children and adolescents were overweight. This means that 14.7 million youngsters in the United States had obesity.

We all know what obesity means. It means someone is overweight, and to define overweight we use the body mass index. BMI is a calculation of a person's body weight in kilograms divided by the square of their height in metres. We use BMI to make categories for children and teens, and we do so on the basis of BMI percentiles. Using percentiles to define BMI categories in children is important because they are still growing.

Please note that percentage and percentile are two different things. A percentage is a number expressed as a fraction of 100. Percentiles, on the other hand, indicate the position of a person by showing what percentage of the reference population has a BMI equal to or greater than theirs. Percentiles are the best clinical indicator to assess growth patterns in children.

For children, obesity is defined as having a body mass index at or above the 95th percentile for age and sex.

Let me give you an example to make things a bit easier to understand. So, we have girl. Let's call her Britney. And she is 12 years old. She is 1.52 metres tall and weighs 61.7 kilograms. Now, we need to calculate her BMI. First, you square the height. That means you multiply 1.52 metres by 1.52 metres. That equals about 2.31 square meters. Next, you divide the weight by the squared height. So, you divide 61.7 kilograms by 2.31. That equals about 26.6. So, Britney's body mass index is 26.6 kg/m². This means her weight is 26.6 kilograms for every square meter of her height.

When we then look at the charts, we see the girl's BMI is at the 96th percentile. In other words, her BMI is the same as or higher than that of 96% of 12-year-old girls in the reference population used to create the growth charts. Britney clearly falls into the obesity category.

This is not surprising, because as a 12-year-old girl she has a 1 in 5 chance of being overweight. What we see is that obesity becomes more of a problem the older we get. In the age group between 2 and 5 years old obesity affects 12.07% of the children. As they grow older, in the 6 to 11 age group, this figure rises to 20.7%. And finally, in the group of the adolescents between 12 and 19 years old, the figure rises to 22.2%. 

Obesity is not only a serious problem for the health of these children, but it is also a burden on our healthcare system. Healthcare for obesity is expensive, for patients and for the healthcare system. In 2021 the estimated annual medical costs of obesity among American children was 1.3 billion dollars. It is a fact that medical costs for children with obesity are higher than for children of a healthy weight. The difference is 116 dollars. In the category of children with severe obesity, the medical costs are even 310 dollars higher per person per year than for children with a healthy weight.

So, the conclusion is that obesity care is expensive and the costs can be direct or indirect. Direct medical costs include treatment that patients or insurers need to pay for. Indirect costs, on the other hand, include reduced work productivity, absenteeism from work, or other aspects related to sickness and death.

For children and adolescents, obesity accounted for 1.3 billion dollars in direct annual medical costs in 2021. That is a serious amount of money. We really need to tackle the problem of obesity at an early age, because as people grow older, obesity becomes an increasingly serious and expensive problem for society. 

In fact, for adults, obesity accounted for 173 billion dollars in direct annual medical costs in 2021. Obesity leads to an additional 1,861 dollars in medical costs per person compared to adults with a healthy weight. In case of severe obesity, the amount even rises to 3,097 dollars. These are the medical costs. 

When we then look at productivity loss, we see that, on average, overweight workers in the United States are absent from their jobs 3 days more per year than workers of a healthy weight. If we translate this absence into specific costs due to obesity, we see a range from 271 dollars to 542 dollars per obese employee, with national productivity losses amounting between 13.4 billion and 26.8 billion dollars in 2019. And 2019 is the last year for which data is available. 

So it's crystal clear: it's time to act now and to prevent obesity among children today. Otherwise, we risk living in a world in which more than 220 million children could be obese by 2040. 

And it wouldn't be me if I didn't have some concrete actions that can be taken. Firstly, I suggest we restrict junk food advertising on television before 9 pm and online at all times. And secondly, we must prevent fast food chains from opening new branches near schools. This is expected to remove up to 7.2 billion calories from children's diets each year. And then we will finally get somewhere. 

It's time to act now, ladies and gentlemen, because the future of our children and grandchildren is in our hands!
