Dear students, dear colleagues.

I would like to start this speech with the story of a British family, a family of 5 from Cornwall. I don't know them personally, I just happened to see their predicament in a BBC documentary. So this family lived in a rented house. Nothing fancy, nothing luxurious, but it was a roof over their heads. Then, out of the blue, the rug was pulled from under their feet: their landlord decided to sell the house. Now to add insult to injury, the new owners bought that house as a holiday home just for themselves, so not even to rent out and make a profit. In other words, the house is actually empty most of the year.

So where did this family of 5 end up? Well, it is heartbreaking, really. They were unable to find an affordable house or apartment and so they were forced to move into a hotel room instead. 5 people in 1 hotel room, packed in like sardines, living out of suitcases. No proper kitchen, just a sink cluttered with baby bottles and washing up. 

Now, this is not just one family's misfortune. This is actually quite a common problem in Cornwall. As it is an idyllic, sunny county, especially the areas near the coast, wealthy people from the stockbroker belt, you know, the rich commuter area around London, are buying properties there as holiday homes. In doing so, they are of course driving up the property prices. So consequently, many of the locals are faced with 2 options. Either they are forced to buy or rent a property elsewhere, out of the area. Or they are forced into temporary accommodation, a hotel room, like the family in my introduction. In some cases, neither option works and people even end up on the streets. 

And it goes without saying that this problem is not limited to just Cornwall. It is only the tip of the iceberg. In England, 140,000 children are currently growing up in temporary accommodation. That is the highest number on record. 

The underlying problem that I'm referring to here is "gentrification". Let me give you a definition first. The very word comes from "gentry," meaning the middle or upper classes. Gentrification is the process where a neighbourhood, typically a poorer or a neglected one, suddenly becomes attractive to people with higher incomes. They move in, renovate houses, open trendy cafés and boutiques. 

At first sight, that can look like a win-win. After all, streets get cleaned up, old buildings get a facelift, infrastructure is improved, and sometimes more parks and green spaces appear. That is not all, new businesses open their doors, boosting employment and investment. A neighbourhood that once had boarded-up windows now has designer lights on in the evening, and yes, that often creates a greater sense of safety too. In short: the neighbourhood becomes a lot more appealing.

But — and here's the rub — there is another side to the coin. The housing prices and the rent go up, which means that long-term residents, often those who have lived there for years or even generations, simply cannot afford to stay. Landlords sell to developers, social housing is demolished or redeveloped, and poorer people bear the brunt of that. They are pushed out. They do not leave because they want to: they leave because they simply cannot afford to stay. They are priced out.

So gentrification can be a double-edged sword. On one hand, renewal and investment; on the other, displacement and exclusion. The poorest people get the short end of the stick. They have no place to go and as I have said, end up in temporary housing or on the streets.

Right, let us call a spade a spade. A house is not just something to buy and sell like apples at a market stall. Groups like Amnesty International argue that housing must be made a legal right, not just a policy goal. Because policies, as we all know, can change with the political wind.

Calling housing a "basic human right" is not just a slogan. We need to realise that without affordable housing, every other right — the right to education, to work, to family life, to health — every other right rests on shaky ground. 

So where does that leave us? The story of this family in Cornwall is not an isolated case, unfortunately. And here is the bottom line: It is time we stopped looking at homes like investment portfolios and it is high time to started treating them like what they are: a human right. 

Because home is the foundation, it is the cornerstone. Without it, everything else wobbles. Work, education, health all depend on having a safe place to live. Without affordable housing, we are basically playing a game of musical chairs and when the music stops, the poorest are left standing. 

Thank you.
