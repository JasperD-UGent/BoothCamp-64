Dear colleagues, dear students.

Today I would like to talk about the tiny house movement. So, first of all, to make sure we are all on the same page, what do we mean when we talk about a tiny home or a tiny house? In simple terms, it is a fully functional living space that is, well, small, like the name suggests, much smaller than a conventional house or apartment. Most definitions place the size somewhere between 10 and 40 or 50 square metres, although as far as I know, there is no strict definition as to the surface. Many tiny houses are built on trailers or wheels, which makes them mobile, to some extent, whereas others are built on a permanent foundation.

You may have noticed that earlier on, I said "fully functioning living space". That is important. Tiny homes are designed to include all the basic functions of a regular home: a place to sleep, a bathroom, a kitchen, and a living area, but they are often combined and carefully planned to save space. So the living room might have a sofa that can be converted into a bed. Or the breakfast corner might double as an office.

What distinguishes tiny homes from simply "small apartments or studios" is the intentional choice to downsize. The focus is usually on using only as much space as is actually needed. So efficiency and multifunctional design are key. For many people, a tiny home is a lifestyle choice. It is about reducing costs, limiting environmental impact, or creating more flexibility, for instance to travel the world. More about that in a minute.

My first contact with the tiny house movement was through a TV show a couple of years ago. I think it was on National Geographic. I remember watching it a couple of times and being intrigued, but it also felt a bit extreme, a bit niche, something you admire from a distance without actually imagining yourself doing it.

Then, a year or so later, it suddenly became more real. An interpreter that I used to practise with decided to build a tiny house and she was asking colleagues and friends if they had a large garden or a field where she could place it. That was the moment when I realised this was not just an American TV concept, it was something people around me were seriously considering and, well, more than just considering, something they were actually doing.

But why? I have very briefly mentioned a few reasons why people opt for a tiny home, but let us look at those reasons, those advantages in a bit more detail.

One of the strongest advantages is lower living costs. Tiny houses cost far less to build or buy, and also to maintain than conventional homes. This allows people to live debt-free or with very little debt.

And that seamlessly brings me to the second advantage. As a result of being debt-free, people are less dependent on full-time work. They can choose to work less and gain more control over their personal time. So in other words, they can spend more time on relationships and hobbies.

Tiny house owners also tend to mention another advantage. They say the tiny home is part of a bigger shift in their lives. A shift from material goods to experiences. Tiny houses force people to reflect on what they truly need. Possessions are reduced. I mean hoarding is not really an option if you only have, say, 20 square metres available. So experiences become more valuable.

And then there is of course the environmental aspect. On a global scale, large houses for everyone are simply unsustainable. Tiny houses can help reduce the carbon footprint, as they use fewer materials and require less energy to heat and power.

That all sounds great, but there are some downsides too. 

First of all, tiny houses are simply not allowed everywhere and the regulations around where you can place them are often unclear.

Secondly, tiny houses are not for everyone or a least not for everyone in every stage of their lives. What I mean by that is that housing needs are not static. As people age, start relationships, have children, or change careers, their space requirements can change significantly. A tiny home that works well at one point in life may become inadequate later.

Dear colleagues, dear students, to conclude, I think it is safe to say we live in a time where bigger is often presented as better. Bigger homes, but also bigger cars, bigger careers, bigger biceps, bigger booties. And we are told that once we reach a certain size, a certain income, a certain lifestyle, then we will feel truly happy. But I think we all know from experience that that kind of happy does not necessarily come, or if it does, it does not last.

The tiny house movement shows us that it is all about priorities and having the guts to ask ourselves: what is actually enough? Having what you need and being honest about what you do not is the first step to real happiness. And not just when it comes to houses.
