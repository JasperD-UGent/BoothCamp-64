Today, I would like to talk to you about the beneficial effects of nature. More specifically, I would like to explain how spending time in nature, going for a walk in the park, for instance, may protect our brains as we grow older.

We often say things like "I need some fresh air" or "I just need to clear my head." And instinctively, many of us turn to nature to do exactly that. After going for a walk in the park or after having a cup of tea in the garden, we feel calmer. After spending a day outdoors, in the countryside or at the seaside, we sleep more deeply. So it kind of goes without saying that nature makes us feel good or makes us feel better, but now, studies suggest that nature may do more than just make us feel better. It may do more than just lift our mood. It may actually reduce the risk of developing serious neurodegenerative disorders, such as Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and other forms of dementia, such as vascular dementia.

So in this speech, I will look at what recent research has found. I will try and explain why nature may have this beneficial, protective effect. And if I have enough time, I will also say a few words about what these findings could mean for the way we design our cities.

Let's start with the study itself. Researchers analysed data from nearly 62 million adults, all aged 65 and over, and all living across the US. Rather than focusing on lifestyle choices, the researchers were interested in a different question: What if where you live matters too? What if that matters just as much as how you live? So, they used satellite imagery to examine where all of these people lived. They were especially interested in their proximity to green and blue spaces. Now, before I move on, just to make sure we are all on the same page: green spaces include things like forests, gardens and parks, whereas blue space includes lakes, rivers, and oceans.

Once the researchers had compiled this information, this environmental information, they compared it with hospital admissions for Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease and related dementias, such as vascular dementia. And, as you will have guessed, the results were striking. The results painted a clear picture. Older adults who lived in areas with more green space were less likely to be hospitalised for all of these conditions. The findings for blue spaces were less straightforward. Blue space was also linked to fewer hospital admissions for Parkinson's disease, but the link was less clear for Alzheimer's and related dementias.

This suggests that simply living near nature — so not even actively seeking it out every day — may have long-term protective effects on the brain. Now, the million-dollar question you might be asking yourselves at this point is: why? Why does nature have this effect? Well, unfortunately, scientists do not have a clear answer just yet, but they do suggest a number of hypotheses, which I will very briefly discuss with you now.

The first explanation is that spending time in nature can reduce our stress levels and we all know that stress, especially chronic stress, is poison for the brain.

Then, there's the link with physical activity and social connection. Having green spaces nearby encourages people to move more and it also brings people together, because parks are places where people meet, talk, and form relationships. And we know from previous studies that both exercise and social interaction help prevent cognitive decline. 

Another explanation why nature reduces the risk of brain diseases is that air pollution and noise levels are lower in green environments and air pollution and constant noise are factors that have been linked to brain damage and an increased risk of dementia. 

Now, this of course raises the question: what if you do not live near nature? Not everyone is fortunate enough to live near forests or parks. Well, if you do not, there is no reason for panic, because experts say that even small amounts of nature help. Even two hours per week in nature has been linked to better health outcomes in other, similar studies.

However, I feel city design should not just be about efficiency, it should also be about public health. Green areas are not just aesthetic extras or luxury features for wealthy neighbourhoods. They are our daily dose of brain protection. If we truly want cities that support healthy ageing, then we need to stop treating nature as an afterthought and we really need to start designing urban environments where green space is not the exception, but the rule.
