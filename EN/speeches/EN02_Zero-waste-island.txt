When I'm at work, I'll also clean up after colleagues and students, present company excluded of course. And when I'm on holiday, I'll even clean up after friends. It's stronger than myself. I daresay I spend quite some time picking up the litter that other people seem to be mindlessly dropping wherever they go. On my way to work, for instance, there's this one highway where some people are constantly fly-tipping. Seeing all those bags of waste scattered along the road drives me absolutely bonkers. So much so that I'll even send e-mail reminders to my local council to let them know when it's time for a massive clean-up again.

So, when I came across a newspaper article reporting on a zero-waste island, I was intrigued. Such a place sounded delightful to me. I wanted to read all about it. And I would like to tell you all about it too. I'm proud to report that this wonderful success story was written in Europe, in the European Union to be precise. The zero-waste island in question is the island of Tilos. It's a tiny Greek island – with only 745 residents – and it's located between Rhodes and Kos in the Aegean Sea.

I think it's safe to say that Tilos has recently undergone a green revolution. The changes obviously didn't happen overnight, they happened gradually, in different stages. First, the island was designated as a national park. Later on, it also became energy self-sufficient, and now, as I've mentioned, it has become the world's first zero-waste island. 

So you could say their zero-waste project is actually the latest chapter in a much bigger story. But it's this last chapter, the zero-waste project, that I would like to elaborate on. 

Before this project was introduced, the island sent a whopping 87 per cent of its waste straight to landfill. You see, Tilos didn't have a separate collection system, at least not a very efficient one. Moreover, the island was under great pressure from increasing numbers of tourists. So at one point, the municipality even considered increasing its landfill capacity. Now that, of course, would have been quite an unsustainable solution, and a pricey one at that, because expanding the landfill and running it would have come with a hefty price tag.

Fortunately though, the municipality decided to explore another avenue and decided to implement a zero-waste project instead. It teamed up with a company, a waste management company that focuses on circular economy solutions. The zero-waste project kicked off in June 2021 and I'll now talk you through the different stages of the project.

First of all, an information centre was set up. It was set up in the port, so residents could learn how to properly sort their waste. They were taught to sort into three categories: one, recyclable materials (that is to say: paper, plastic, metal, aluminium and glass); two, organic waste (so food, fruit or vegetable peels, stones and bones); and three, non-recyclable materials (such as soiled paper or personal items like razors and masks).

Next, each household was given its own zero-waste equipment. They received boxes for recyclable and non-recyclable waste, a bag for organic waste, and even a box for cigarette butts.

Then, regular door-to-door collections were implemented across the island. 

And after a while, the project even started diverting all residual waste away from the original landfill site. In other words, all the waste on the island's landfill was somehow recycled or reused. 

Now, the scheme has proved such a success that the island no longer has any rubbish bins and the landfill site has now closed completely. Instead of a typical landfill, Tilos now has a centre, a Centre for Creative Upcycling, where items can be repaired, reused, or turned into art supplies or building materials.

So the island now stands as a shining example of how sustainable practices can have a profound impact both on the environment and on the local community. I realise that may sound a bit vague to you, so let me give you a few examples.

I'll first give you an example of the impact the project has had on the environment. Waste management and recycling initiatives have led to a significant reduction in plastic. This significantly helps to protect the island's delicate ecosystems and marine life. So this project helps to rid our oceans of plastic, by avoiding that more plastic ends up in the oceans in the first place.

And then an example of the impact on the local community to wrap this up. The project has already created new job opportunities related to eco-tourism. It has brought an influx of environmentally-conscious visitors to the island, which is boosting local businesses. 

Ladies and gentlemen, if you are considering a trip to Tilos to see with your own eyes what a zero-waste island looks like, don't forget that visitors have to take part in the scheme as well. I guess I don't need to tell you where I'm headed on my next holiday.
