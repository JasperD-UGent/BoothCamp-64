Dear colleagues, dear students, I'll be talking about spiders today. Many people dislike spiders, but these eight-legged creatures really don't deserve their bad reputation, which is mainly based on misunderstandings and myths. In reality, arachnids are stunningly diverse, ingenious creatures and they have so many characteristics and behaviours worth admiring. 

Despite their bad reputation, spiders are phenomenal ecosystem engineers and they're responsible for keeping hundreds of thousands of insects and agricultural pests in check. All the world's spiders combined eat between 400 and 800 million tons of insects every year. Studies convincingly argue that, in some ecosystems, more than 40 percent of all insect biomass passes through spiders, making them the number one controllers of insect populations.

If some arachnophobic magician could wave a magic wand to make all spiders disappear, that would be the greatest ecological catastrophe that ever happened. So to give spiders the credit they deserve, let's try to debunk five unflattering rumours or myths that surround them.

Myth number 1: "spiders are out to bite humans". Well, that is just plainly wrong! In fact, most people will never be bitten by a spider in their lifetime, because spiders are simply not interested in interacting with humans. Out of the 45 000 species roaming the planet, very few ever come in contact with us. They're not bloodsucking creatures so they don't actively seek us out, unlike mosquitoes, ticks or bedbugs.

Myth number 2: "we swallow spiders in our sleep". Throughout the years, several online forums and publications have claimed that we swallow as many as 8 spiders in our sleep every single year, but there is no verified study, photo, collected specimen, medical record, or proven observation of a spider either crawling or attempting to crawl into a person's mouth. In fact, spiders want nothing to do with our smelly mouths cracked open for heavy breathing and snoring while we sleep. The air current leaving a sleeping person's mouth actually disturbs the little sensory hairs that are all over their body and scares them off. 

Myth number 3: "Spiders lay eggs in bananas and other fruits". Well, it is true that spiders can occasionally be found in banana processing warehouses and banana shipments or they can be found crawling out of the crates in which the fruit has travelled. This is because a pack of bananas, grapes, or other clustered fruits, constitute an ideal place to hide a sac of tiny eggs.

However these eggs would be visible and hanging outside the fruit, not be tucked inside it, as no single spider makes holes inside fruit to lay its eggs there, let alone cause the fruit to swell, tremble, and then explode with thousands of spiders once the eggs hatch, as is sometimes shown in implausible horror movies. Some burrowing spiders do dig out a home inside the dirt, but even they cannot pierce through something as tough as fruits or plants.

Myth number 4, related to myth number 3: "Spiders can lay eggs under your skin". The story here usually goes something like this: a woman returns from a holiday in a warm, exotic location and finds a frightening bump on her cheek that's pulsating and growing. Concerned, she visits a doctor, and when the specialist pries the bump open, hundreds of small spiders crawl out. It's one of the world's most commonly believed urban legends, but the story actually never happened. The whole myth is based on a fictional German short story from the 1840s, where a boil on a woman's cheek bursts open with spiders because she's made a pact with the Devil.

So rest assured, spiders don't have the means or even the interest to burrow into thick layers of human skin and lay their eggs there, and in the extremely rare case that a spider does bite a human, it would only inject a small dose of mostly innocuous venom with its tiny fangs, not lay eggs.

And last but not least, myth number 5: "when it gets cold outside, spiders crawl into your house up drains and sewers". This is a very common misunderstanding, so let me set the record straight by giving you some basic ecological facts. Some spider species have evolved to live outside and others have evolved to live inside. These different species will only very rarely meet. Outdoor spiders might wander in accidentally, but they have no interest whatsoever in finding permanent refuge in our cozy homes.

About 95 percent of the spiders you see indoors were always indoors; they hatched indoors; they grew up indoors. They belong to a small number of species that have been living around human-made buildings for as long as human-made buildings have existed. That is why chasing and catching spiders in your abode and trying to set them free into the wild is counterproductive.

The spider venturing in your bathtub or near your sink is probably a house spider trying to get a drink, since water sources inside the home are few and far between. That individual was already living in your house, and it was most probably crawling towards the drain. Plus, most plumbing pipes have a sediment-trapping section constantly replete with water that most spiders wouldn't be able to cross anyway.

So, dear colleagues, let me just conclude by saying that next time you see a spider in your house, remember it's closer to a roommate than an intruder.

Thank you for your attention.
