Dear students, dear colleagues.

A few years ago, I had an exchange student from Spain in one of my grammar classes, Sofia. I came to know her as a very intelligent, ambitious, and hard-working student. We kept in touch after she returned to Spain. As soon as she had her translation degree under her belt, she started working at a tech company in Madrid. One day, she was chatting with a coworker. A male coworker. And she discovered that he was earning significantly more than she was. And just to be absolutely clear: they both had the same role at the company, they had the same level of experience and they both had positive performance reviews. She was simply paid less because she is a woman.

Now, it goes without saying that Sofia's story is not unique. Unfortunately, we know that there are many similar stories throughout the European Union. And that brings me to today's subject: the gender pay gap in the European Union.

In this speech, I will tell you what the gender pay gap is and why it persists to this day. I will also talk about some of the progress that has been made so far, and about the challenges that lie ahead.

So first things first. What is the gender pay gap? If we look at how much women earn per hour and how much men earn per hour, on average, of course, then the difference between those 2 hourly earnings is the gender pay gap. 

Now, 2 remarks, just to be a bit more precise.

Firstly, the gap is calculated using gross hourly earnings. In other words, before income tax and before social security contributions are deducted. 

And secondly, only companies of 10 or more employees are taken into account in these calculations.

At this point, you will probably be wondering exactly how big the gap is. Well, I looked up EU figures, or rather, I tried to. Unfortunately, both on the Commission website and on the Parliament website, the most recent figures are about 2021.

In 2021, women in the EU earned on average 12.7% less per hour than men. That means that for every euro a man earns, a woman earns just 87 cents. 

That's the EU average, of course. As you can imagine, the gender pay gap varies widely across the EU. 

On the one hand, there are countries like Hungary, Germany, Austria, Latvia and Estonia, where the gap is higher than 17%. 

On the other hand, we have countries like Romania, Belgium and Italy, where the gap is under 5%. 

However, we need to be very careful comparing those statistics, because a lower pay gap doesn't always mean more equality. In some countries, fewer women work overall, and those who do are often better educated, which may skew the numbers.

This being said, why do women still tend to earn less?

First, there's horizontal or sectoral segregation, that is to say that women are overrepresented in lower-paying sectors such as health care and education. About 30% of women work in these sectors, compared to just 8% of men. Meanwhile, women are underrepresented in male-dominated, higher-paying sectors such as tech, finance or engineering. This structural division accounts for around 24% of the gender pay gap.

Second, about 28 % of women in the EU are employed part-time, compared to only 8 % of men. The cause or the result, the chicken or the egg, is an unequal share of paid and unpaid work. Women often work more hours per week than men when you count both paid and unpaid work. But they spend significantly more time on unpaid tasks, like caregiving and household duties. That affects their career choices and their earnings.

Third, no speech about the pay gap is complete without a reference to the glass ceiling or vertical segregation. Regardless of the sector they are in, women are less likely to reach leadership roles. Less than 1 in 10 CEOs of top companies in the EU are women. 

And last but not least, pay discrimination, like the case of Sophia in my introduction, still exists. In some cases, women earn less than men for doing equal work or work of equal value, even though the principle of equal pay is enshrined in the European Treaties since 1957. 

Knowing the problem and the causes is one thing; tackling them is another. So, what is the EU doing? 

The EU is pushing for pay transparency and encourages companies to disclose salary structures and ensure fairness. 

The EU is also promoting equal sharing of parental leave, better childcare services, and flexible working arrangements. 

That's all well and good, but we will need a culture change. We need to challenge gender stereotypes about who does what kind of work. 

Dear students, colleagues. Let me end where I began, with Sofia. She eventually spoke up. Her company reviewed its pay structure and made changes. But she had to fight for what should have been normal. I hope the next generation doesn't have to any more. 

Thank you.
