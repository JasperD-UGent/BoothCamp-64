Do you remember the heat waves that we experienced last summer? I certainly do. I remember I was in the south of France, where temperatures soared to 42 degrees Centigrade. Now, if you know me, you know that I really love soaking up the sun. But those temperatures, they were just unbearable. As soon as you stepped outside, you just felt as if you were suffocating. Breathing normally was simply not possible. And obviously, my holiday destination was not even the hottest place in the world this summer. In the US, temperatures exceeded 50 degrees Centigrade.

Now, it goes without saying that these heatwaves resulted in all sorts of problems. 

The first consequence that comes to mind are perhaps wildfires, as heatwaves create ideal conditions for wildfires. That is because they dry out vegetation so much it becomes highly flammable. Last summer, we witnessed huge wildfires in Spain and Greece, for instance.

But that is not all, for heatwaves can also lead to heat-related illnesses, such as heat stroke. Prolonged exposure to extreme heat can even lead to an increase in mortality, particularly among vulnerable people, such as the elderly, young children, and those with pre-existing health conditions.

However, there is another critical issue connected to heatwaves: water shortages. High temperatures can accelerate the evaporation of water from reservoirs and water bodies. That, in turn, triggers water shortages and often requires restrictions on water usage.

As a result of rising temperatures due to climate change, water supplies are becoming increasingly scarce, which causes more and more cities to panic about their water supplies, or lack thereof. And so to try and come up with solutions, they are turning to a pioneer in the field of water management. 

They are turning to the driest country in Sub-Saharan Africa: Namibia. You see, Namibia is home to not one, but two of the world's most ancient deserts: the Kalahari and the Namib deserts. The capital, Windhoek, is sandwiched between the two, nearly 650 km away from the nearest river and almost 500 km away from the coast. It's quite hard to imagine life thriving in Windhoek, and yet half a million people call it home, 99 per cent of whom have access to potable water thanks to a revolutionary technology that was pioneered there. It's this technology that I would like to talk about in a bit more detail. 

In the 1950s, Windhoek struggled to cope with a rapidly growing population, and severe water shortages gripped the city. So, there just wasn't enough water for the growing population. But, as is often the case, disaster forced innovation, and in 1968 Windhoek became the first place in the world to produce drinking water directly from sewage. Yes, you have heard that right: sewage, or waste water, is transformed into drinking water. 

That may sound revolting, but, as it turns out, drinking it is completely safe. The water treatment facility has never been linked to an outbreak of any waterborne disease. So how is sewage then turned into drinking water? 

Well, first of all, I really want to stress that the process has undergone quite some changes since its introduction in 1968. 

The treatment facility of course eliminates pollutants (so things like antibiotics, preservatives from cosmetics, or anti-corrosion chemicals from dishwasher tablets). 
Needless to say that it also removes pathogens from human waste. It does that by harnessing bacteria. This process kind of mimics what happens when water is recycled in nature, but Windhoek does it all much, much quicker than Mother Nature. The water is made drinkable in under 24 hours, whereas nature typically requires years.

Additionally, online instruments are used to monitor the water continuously. Staff ensure that drinking water is only sent to homes if it meets specific guidelines set by the World Health Organisation. If any inconsistencies are detected, the plant goes into recycle mode. Distribution is then halted until correct values are restored. 

This water treatment plant now produces close to 21 million litres of drinking water every day, which represents 35 per cent of the city's consumption. As you can see, Namibians just couldn't survive without it. 

As water shortages grip the planet, Windhoek's insights are more important than ever. So, not unsurprisingly, superpowers across the globe have taken an interest in this water treatment process. Over the past few years, delegations from the US, France, Germany, Australia, and Singapore have all visited Windhoek.

You might be wondering why they are not using this method already. I mean, these are all wealthy countries. Surely, they have the money and the know-how to build one of these facilities. Well, the only thing keeping them from doing so is public or political opposition. Disgust is a very powerful emotion. And, clearly, the idea of 'toilet to tap' undermines the potential. Not everyone can cope with the idea of drinking water that just 24 hours earlier was still toilet water. But we will probably have to get used to that idea if we are to become more climate resilient.
