Today, I would like to talk about bats and echolocation. Echolocation is indeed a remarkable skill in bats, but humans have also developed their own version of echolocation. It uses sound to help perceive and understand the environment. Bats have evolved echolocation over millions of years. Humans have developed similar technology to do the same barely 100 years ago. Today I want to delve deeper into the intricacies of echolocation. I will talk about its biological origins in bats and about the application of sonar technology in submarines.

Right, let's begin with the bats. First and foremost, bats are mammals. They are the only mammals capable of sustained flight. Their wings are actually elongated fingers that are covered in a thin membrane which enables them to maneuver through the air. There are over 1400 bat species worldwide. And they come in very different shapes and sizes. Sizes vary from 3 cm for the smallest to 33 cm for the largest species. Believe it or not, but bats are also very useful creatures. They pollinate flowers, disperse seeds and keep insect populations in check. 

Most bats are nocturnal creatures. They hunt for food in the dark. This brings me to one of their most remarkable features and the main topic of this speech: echolocation. You see, bats emit high-frequency calls through their mouths or noses, and listen for the returning echoes bouncing off objects in their path. This enables them to navigate, to locate prey, and to effectively communicate in the dark. You see, bats interpret the time delay and intensity of the echoes to create a mental map of their surroundings. And a very precise map at that! Did you know that some bats can locate an object as small as the width of a single human hair? With this unique ability bats are able to live in a wide range of habitats. Some species use echolocation to navigate open deserts. Others exploit echolocation to navigate dense forests.

In the realm of human technology, echolocation is the basis for sonar systems. As you probably all know, sonar is used by submarines for underwater navigation, detection, and communication. Sonar technology was invented over 100 years ago. The first patent for sonar technology was granted in the US in 1913. It is said that the sinking of the Titanic in 1911 sparked research into echolocation. You all know that the Titanic crashed into an iceberg on its first journey across the Ocean. Well, it actually crashed into the part of the iceberg that couldn't be seen because it was below the water level. Now, if the Titanic had been equipped with a sonar system, that submerged part of the iceberg could have been spotted long before it would become a real threat. 

Sonar operates on similar principles to those of bat echolocation. But it is made to meet the specific challenges of underwater exploration. Submarines emit sound pulses into the surrounding water at frequencies that we humans cannot perceive. The sound waves then travel through the water and reflect off all kinds of objects. Think of the seabed, other vessels or marine life. The reflected waves are then picked up by the submarine's sensors. Sonar operators analyse the time it takes for these sound waves to return. They also analyse their intensity. In this way, the operators can create detailed maps of the environment. And this allows them to detect potential threats in even the most challenging conditions.

As I have mentioned before, both bats and submarines use echolocation for navigation and perception. There are, however, some differences between its biological and technological versions. Bats have anatomical structures and processing systems that have evolved over millions of years to optimize their abilities. Submarines on the other hand, rely on hardware and software to both generate and interpret sonar signals. As a result, there are differences in the range and frequencies of the waves that are used. Sonars operate between approximately 30 kHz and 500 kHz. Most bat calls range from 9 kHz to 200 kHz. And by comparison: humans can only hear in the frequency range from 20 Hz to up to 20 kHz.

Now, the study of echolocation, whether in bats or submarines, has profound implications for several fields like biology, neuroscience, engineering and oceanography. However, what I didn't know is that echolocation also intersects with AI. And as you all know I am particularly interested in AI. For example, some researchers believe that the combination of AI and echolocation could provide an alternative to image recognition. Currently, image recognition relies solely on visual input. But visual input requires light. In environments where light is lacking, sound waves could help us out. They could be used to identify objects in environments where cameras can't. So they could be used in new surveillance systems that can do a lot more than current technologies.

It is clear that using sound to navigate and to perceive the world holds many promises for the future. So, ladies and gentlemen, I am not going to conclude by saying "you ain't seen nothing yet". The right thing to say is "you ain't heard nothing yet".
