Specifically, my research looks at the implications for animal welfare in growing dairy herds. How can we ensure they have a life worth living? Coming from a farming background, I have always been interested in our relationship with the animals in our care.

How can we reconcile our desire to treat them well, look after them and keep them happy? With the fact that we also eat them. As an animal welfare scientist, I am interested in what we need to give our cows the best possible life. What do they want to do? What do they like? How can we give them what they want? 

Humans often make sacrifices to achieve outcomes that make them feel good. Anyone who plays sport, works, studies or saves up for a holiday is making a sacrifice. Interestingly, in the human world, the magnitude of the sacrifice made is often proportional to the respect or reward received. We think it's fantastic when humans make big sacrifices. However, in the field of animal welfare, this is not the case. Our aim is to minimise the sacrifices that animals must make. 

It is clear to anyone who has watched cows grazing that they enjoy the taste of grass. However, it is important to consider the sacrifices they make to do so and whether these sacrifices are worthwhile.

Today, I'm going to talk about animal welfare. It is important to many people, but what is it, and how do we measure it? I'd like to talk about two concepts today: animal welfare and animal ethics. OK, let's start with the first concept. We worry about animal welfare because we want animals to be happy. Animal welfare is about how the animal feels. It's a science that looks at how animals perceive their own condition. But how do we measure good animal welfare? 

We can observe cows at a given moment and determine whether they appear happy. That's one way to assess their happiness. But animal welfare scientists add up the sum of life experiences. They assess whether animals have had a life worth living overall. They use a scale where one end represents a good life and the other end a life not worth living. We could also have a life that is neither good nor bad. Or we could have a life that is not worth living. Or, indeed, a life worth avoiding. Animal welfare science is therefore all about measuring things about animals. It's about understanding how they feel and how they perceive their own condition. It is a science. 

This is different from animal ethics, which concerns how humans feel about what we do to and with animals. Our beliefs about animal ethics are based on our life experience and our cultural norms. Some people think it's OK to eat horses, or dogs, or whales. Some people think it's not OK to eat any of those things. Indeed, some people think it's not OK to eat cows in places where they're sacred, or pigs. For some people, it's not OK to eat the products of any living being. 

You know, the traditional view of animal welfare scientists is that we don't give much thought to why an animal is where it is. It's all about how the animal feels. For example, a dog bred for food in Vietnam has the same animal welfare requirements as a pet dog in the Hunter Valley outside Sydney. They need a good life and a humane death. Whether or not we should be breeding dogs for food is an ethical issue. 

Animal welfare is about how the animal feels. Animal ethics is about how we feel. Both are important. Let's talk about animal welfare. 

Arguments in this field often focus on the extent to which we should avoid suffering. Indeed, most of Australia's cruelty laws are precisely about that: the prevention of unnecessary suffering. An important word there is 'unnecessary'. Because some suffering is necessary in life. 
When I reflect on my own life, I realise that I have experienced moments of great anguish, terrible sadness, and yes, extreme pain. But I've also experienced great joy, pleasure, and happiness. For the record, I do think my life has been very worthwhile thus far. So a good life will likely include some terrible moments. 

In some respects, I think farm animals in our care are often luckier than wild animals. And indeed humans, when it comes to a humane death, because we have euthanasia. Look at nature: not many ducks die in their nest surrounded by their loving family. They die of starvation, predation, disease, injury or some horrible combination of these things. If they were in our care, we would not consider it humane. 

However, animal welfare is about more than just avoiding suffering. If we want animals to have a good life, we need to understand what they want from life.
