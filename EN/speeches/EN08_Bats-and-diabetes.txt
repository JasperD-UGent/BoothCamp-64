Well, unlike us humans, these flying mammals can do that without developing negative health consequences such as diabetes. With diabetes, as I'm sure you know, the body can't produce enough insulin or can't use the insulin as well as it should, which results in too much sugar in the bloodstream. 

Fruit bats, however, have a genetic system that controls their blood sugar levels without fail. Genetic adaptations help to keep their sugary diets from becoming harmful. It goes without saying that researchers would like to learn from that to make better insulin-therapies for people. 

So to figure out how these bats can eat so much sugar and thrive, a team of researchers focused on the evolution of the fruit bat's kidneys and pancreas. The pancreas, which is located just behind the stomach, plays a big role in digestion. You see, during digestion, the pancreas makes pancreatic juices called enzymes. I'm sure you remember from your biology lessons in secondary school that these enzymes break down starches, fats, and sugars. But that's not all, the pancreas also produces hormones that regulate your blood sugar levels. 

So back to the fruit bat's pancreas and kidneys. Researchers compared the fruit bat with an insect-eating bat and what they found was that the pancreas and kidneys in fruit bats have evolved, have evolved to accommodate their sugary diet. They've evolved in three specific ways. First, the pancreas has more cells to produce insulin. It also has more cells that produce another sugar-regulating hormone, one that is perhaps less well-know. It's called glucagon. And then lastly, the fruit bat kidneys have more cells to trap scarce salts and electrolytes as they filter blood. 

So, in short, the organization of DNA is very different between those two bat species, between the fruit bats and the insect-eating bats. While some of the fruit bat's biology resembles what is found in humans with diabetes, the bats don't seem to have the same health problems as humans.
