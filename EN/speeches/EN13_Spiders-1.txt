Dear colleagues, dear students.

Today's topic is spiders. Now, the mere mention of the word "spiders" is bound to make several of you feel very uncomfortable. After all, 3 to 6 percent of people worldwide, especially women, are believed to suffer from arachnophobia, an extreme fear of spiders, a fear so intense they simply cannot think of anything else and cannot function properly. Before I elaborate on this deep-rooted fear of arachnids, and particularly on its origin, let's start with some basic objective spider facts. 

Our planet is home to more than 45,000 known species of spiders. They live in habitats all over the world, from lush rainforests to arid deserts. These eight-legged creatures, which belong to the same class of animals as scorpions and ticks, come in many sizes. While some are so small you can barely see them, others are up to 13 centimetres large. Though all spiders secrete venom to one degree or another, only a handful, like the black widow, pose a real danger to humans.

What about their diets? Most spider species are carnivorous, eating other insects, but since spiders are unable to swallow their food as it is, they inject their prey with digestive fluids, and then suck out the liquefied remains. It is estimated that one specimen can eat as many as 2,000 insects in only one year. They eat many different insects, including mosquitoes that can transmit deadly diseases like malaria, dengue, and yellow fever. So spiders benefit humans in that they effectively help prevent mosquito-borne diseases from spreading. 

These little creatures also benefit humans by consuming agricultural pests feeding on the fruits, leaves, stems, and seeds of crops that we harvest for food, pests such as aphids, grasshoppers, and beetles. Without spiders, the human food supply would be at serious risk. 

Now that we have firmly established that spiders are not just creepy, but are actually useful, let's go back to the grueling topic of arachnophobia. Many of us will quickly jump on a chair at the sight of a spider. But if they are so useful, then why do they cause such panic? Well, scientists have suggested that the biological fear of spiders is passed on through our genes, because spiders are able to seriously hurt humans. 

But that logic sounds a bit flimsy. As pointed out before, the vast majority of spiders are harmless. Out of about 38,000 species, only 0.1 to 0.3 percent pose an actual threat to humans. Spider bites rarely cause death, even in remote places like South America and Australia, which contain some of the world's largest and most dangerous specimens. So, personally, I am not buying this speculative idea of arachnophobia being encoded in our genes. 

To be totally honest, my own reaction at the sight of a spider mostly depends on other people's presence. If alone, I will just get rid of it myself, no fuss, but if there are other people around, I will likely make a hysterical scene, screaming and begging someone to come to my rescue. 

However, spiders did not particularly bother me as a little kid. But then, later on, in secondary school, whenever a creepy crawly entered the room, at least one person would go bananas. So my own hysterical reaction – and I daresay the way many other people react – is very much an acquired behaviour. And just the other day, I came across two interesting and conclusive experiments that prove my hypothesis. 

In one study, toddlers were brought into a lab space with four fun toys and four live animals—a snake, a tarantula, a hamster, and a fish. The researchers figured that if fear of spiders and snakes is innate, the toddlers should spend less time interacting with them than with the other animals and the toys. However, the participating toddlers all loved the animals, all the animals, including the allegedly fearsome snake and tarantula. They spent almost twice as much time interacting with them as with the toys. So much for an innate fear. The only evidence of fear the researchers noticed was in the toddlers' parents, who sometimes would hastily pull their children away from the snake and spider. 

So it seems like spider fear is indeed inherited, but not through our genes. Parents seem to pass it on in other ways – by their own fearful reactions or in the way they talk about spiders. The researchers therefore ran a second experiment. They recorded parents and children talking about spiders and other animals as they walked through a local zoo. They found that parents used substantially more negative and threatening language when talking about spiders compared to when they were talking about other species. Crucially, research consistently shows that negative or threatening language can cause children to develop anxieties. So arachnophobia seems to be cultural, not congenital.

Dear colleagues, dear students, surely we've all run into more than a few spiders in our lives so far and we will all run into many, many more. As there's really no point in mimicking other people's fear, let's just be grateful for those creepy crawlies and consider them friends. After all, they eat the one critter I hate even more than spiders: mosquitos. The enemy of my enemy is my friend.

Thank you for your attention.
