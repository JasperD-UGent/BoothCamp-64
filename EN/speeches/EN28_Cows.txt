Ladies and gentlemen.

Today, I would like to address those who claim that farming is harmful and that cattle farming is cruel simply because we ultimately slaughter the animals. In the next few minutes, I will focus on dairy farms, where cows are kept for milk production.

Actually, I can relate to the behaviour of cows because, just like me, they like to spend most of the day eating. Although, I'm not quite as fond of the taste of grass as they are. But, all jokes aside, cows have three very well-ingrained instincts. Instincts and the need to exhibit certain behaviours are evolutionary traits. Humans have been around for 2 or 3 million years, but herd behaviour in our ancestral animals has likely existed for more than 200 million years.

The first instinct I would like to mention is that cows are herd animals. Cows like to be in a herd because they find comfort in belonging to one and having an important place within it. This makes them feel safe. 

A cow separated from the herd suffers great mental anguish – you may have seen videos of a cow off by itself behaving irrationally, such as running through fences. Similarly, humans who are disconnected from their social group suffer great mental pain. People have even killed themselves as a result of being separated from their community. The same goes for cows – the desire for herd approval is so strong that it can override good judgement and reason.

Interestingly, there are two types of animals in the world: prey animals and hunter animals. Cows are prey animals and tend to form large groups for protection. Humans, on the other hand, are hunters. They tend to form tribes that often fight each other. One of the things I really like about cows is that they don't fight or become aggressive just because a cow is from a different herd or looks different. Unlike humans, they don't start wars over territory or identity.

The second important instinct is agency. Animal welfare scientists use the term 'agency' to describe what we would probably call 'freedom': the ability to choose what you are doing and to have some control over your own life.

I think cows in a paddock do have some agency, in that they can choose when to sit or stand, who to interact with, and when to eat. However, true agency – the ability to choose who they mate with – is a sacrifice they must make simply because they live on a farm. After all, freedom always comes with strings attached. But in return, cows are well fed, cared for and guaranteed a humane death. Therefore, I actually think this is a sacrifice well worth making.

A third instinct that needs to be mentioned is that, like us, cows are creatures of habit, through and through. They love routine and, once accustomed to the milking routine, for instance, a nice, consistent routine is one of the ways we can provide them with good welfare.

In this way, cows are able to express some of their natural behaviours in captivity. However, animal welfare science is not just about preventing suffering. Let's not forget it's also about ensuring a humane death. Now, you might wonder: How can we reconcile providing animals with good welfare while also killing and eating them? Let's address the elephant in the room.

From an animal welfare science point of view, this is interesting because a humane death without unnecessary suffering is a good thing. Whether or not death should occur is, once again, an ethical issue.

Personally, I'm reasonably comfortable with that. However, I might feel differently if I thought cows had an expectation of the future. I don't believe they spend their days looking forward to the day their granddaughter joins the herd, nor do I think you'll find them drawing up five-year plans.

Cows remember the past, but they live in the present, and I think many of us humans would be happier if we did the same.

So, for me, farming is not all doom and gloom. We should celebrate the biodiversity that can result from it. After all, our cows coexist with birds, snakes, trees, and all kinds of other wildlife, albeit at lower levels than if there were no farms.

I should say I'm content with the effects of farming, where cows can choose to walk or sit for most of the day, be part of a herd, and experience the satisfaction of social interaction. They can experience moments of pleasure, even though there may also be moments of pain and suffering. They can experience contentment, even if not 100 per cent of the time. They can have a life worth living. While I respect the right of people to hold this view, I disagree with those who believe that we should not farm animals, particularly those who argue that we should not have them in the first place because we have to kill them.

I worry that this philosophy is misguided because death and suffering still occur in the production of food crops and plant-based fibres; they're just one step further out of sight

I believe that the world would be a better place if at the end of the day we all focused on maximising happiness and minimising suffering. We shouldn't just try to avoid death.

Thank you for your attention.
