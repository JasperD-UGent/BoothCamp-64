Ladies and gentlemen, dear students.

I'll start with a very personal bit of information: I don't have kids, nor do I have any desire to have kids in the future. So, to put it bluntly, I couldn't care less about them. Now, most of my closest friends do have kids. And so, unfortunately for me, there's no escaping some baby-related chit-chat. I've had to sit through hours' worth of breastfeeding horror stories and the finer financial points of child-care facilities. 

However, there's one type of baby-related stories that don't completely bore me to tears. It's the silly anecdotes about how mums and dads handle things differently, very very differently. You've all heard versions of these stories. Mum worked from home yesterday. She answered all her e-mails, prepared a 2-hour presentation complete with a nifty PowerPoint presentation. All while looking after the baby, decluttering the house and doing the laundry. And then in the evening, she also cooked a healthy meal from scratch and went for a workout. Dad, on the other hand, looked after the baby for half a day during the weekend. By the time mum came home from a shopping spree, the whole house looked like a battle-field. 

I'm sure you've heard versions of these. But I sometimes wonder if these hackneyed clichés aren't doing more harm than good. I mean, hearing them, it's no wonder that so many men are reluctant to take paternity leave. It really sounds like they can't do anything right!

In this speech, I'd like to talk about paternity leave. Let me start by stating the obvious: it is now more widely available than ever. I'll give you some figures to corroborate that. 

In 1994, only 40 countries had statutory paternity leave provisions. By now, that number has gone up to more than 90. So the number of countries where it is enshrined in law has more than doubled in the last few decades.

And that's not all. Globally, at least 4 out of every 10 organisations are thought to provide paid leave above the statutory minimum. 

So, as I've said, paternity leave is more widely available than ever and yet, the proportion of men actually taking it remains very low. Let me give you an example. In 2015, the UK introduced a new parental leave policy. But of all the fathers who were eligible to take advantage of the policy, only about 1% did. 

And why is that? Well, overall, there are two main reasons why so many men are still hesitant to take paternity leave. One reason is fear of professional discrimination. Most men say they are afraid of being discriminated against professionally. In other words, they worry about missing out on pay rises and promotions. Another reason is mockery. Apparently, some men are scared of being ridiculed by their coworkers and bosses.

Now, these concerns are rooted in gender stereotypes. I think it's worth pointing out that while gender roles at work have changed – have changed for the better – in the last few decades, some stereotypes have persisted. Let me give you two examples of what I mean.

Women are no longer seen as less competent than men, but they continue to be seen as more nurturing and more caring and, in turn, as more suitable for roles that require these attributes, such as childcare. Men, on the other hand, continue to be seen as more decisive, more assertive, more competitive. These stereotypes can lead men to internalize the belief that they are simply not suited for childcare and so they may be discouraged from taking paternity leave.

Another stereotype that has persisted is that men should prioritize work over family. So men who do take parental leave can face backlash. They may be seen as weak, lacking work commitment and so on, which can result in consequences at work such as being demoted. 

So how can we tackle this problem? Well, I think there is a need for role models, to show that taking parental leave is normal and acceptable for men too. Ideally those role models should have leadership positions, to clearly demonstrate that you can be successful professionally even if you take parental leave. Unfortunately, however, there's evidence that it's precisely these men, the ones at the highest echelons, who tend to take the least leave. 

Ladies and gentlemen, it's of course great if states or companies offer paid parental leave for fathers, but as long as men think they might be penalised or mocked for making use of such policies, not much will change. 

So I hope we'll see more of these great role models soon. And to just get back to the silly supermum versus clumsy dad jokes I mentioned at the very beginning. Perhaps women should think twice before taking cheap shots at the expense of their partners. You might not think your partner's a great multitasker. He might not do everything perfectly, that is to say he might not do things your way, but cut him some slack. He might just be the role model another man needs.

Thank you for your attention.
