Dear students, dear colleagues.

I'd like to talk about something that rubs me the wrong way: people wasting perfectly good food. You see, quite often my elderly neighbours will say things like "Oh, we never eat the same food two days in a row. So any leftovers just go into the bin." Or they will say "Oh, our kids dropped off some food, but what they prepared isn't something we'd usually have, so we threw it all away." 

Really, the amount of food they throw away every week is mind-blowing to say the least and they're not the only people throwing away food without giving it any second thought, as one-third of all food produced globally is never eaten. Around 9.5 million tonnes of food are thrown away each year in the UK alone. Which in and by itself is awful, but it becomes even more heart-breaking when you realise that more than 25 million tonnes of greenhouse gas emissions were released for the production of all that wasted food. And what makes it even more harrowing is that meanwhile, more than 7 million people in the UK go hungry, because as I'm sure you know, food poverty in the UK has reached unprecedented levels.

Now, in all fairness, I should point out that the UK has cut food waste by around 15% in the last 15 years, but I daresay bolder actions are needed. So in the next few minutes, I'll discuss six solutions to limit food waste. However, I will be focussing on the food industry rather than individual households, like my awfully wasteful neighbours.

Okay, so, first of all, knowledge is power. What I mean by that is, if you are not keeping track of how much food you waste, if you are not measuring how much you waste, then you don't know how big your waste problems are. Apparently, around 40% of the 'top 50 food companies' in the UK are not measuring food waste, which is bound to have a massive financial impact as well. It's not until companies start measuring, they realise they haven't got their house in order, which gives them a financial incentive to do something about it.

A second solution is to make the food industry more circular: the idea is to create business partnerships, so as to turn one company's waste into a useful resource for another. After all, one man's waste is another man's treasure. 

Now, let me illustrate that with an example: there's a British craft beer that is made from leftover loaves of bread and what is more, the profits don't go to shareholders, but to environmental charities.

Another solution is to improve storage and transport. According to a study by the UN, 14% of all food is lost after harvesting, but before it reaches shops, which is a particular problem in lower-income countries, as they tend to lack the necessary infrastructure to keep perishable food fresh during transport and storage. 

Thankfully, several organisations have come up with inexpensive, low-tech solutions. One example is an edible oil that adds a protective layer to fruit and vegetables, keeping them fresher for longer.

Okay, so far so good. Let's move on to solution number four: incentivising farmers to harvest crops. You see, letting excess crops rot in the ground is currently more cost-effective for farmers than spending resources on harvesting them. 

Therefore, charity organisations are pushing the British government to offer financial incentives for farmers to harvest surplus food and deliver it to those in need, which should help to tackle hunger.

While solution number 4 was the proverbial carrot, solution number 5 is the stick. What I mean by that is that rather than incentivising businesses to reduce waste, some countries have taken the opposite approach by penalising organic waste. 

A good example of that is South Korea's capital, Seoul, where residents are effectively charged for the amount of food waste they produce, either through the purchase of mandatory waste bags (the more you use, the more you pay) or through automated bins, which charge by weight. Such schemes have seen the city's food recycling levels rocket from a lowly 2% back in 1995 to around 95% today. 

Another example of the stick approach is France, which in 2016, became the first country to ban supermarkets from throwing away edible food that could still be donated to charity, introducing fines of up to €75,000 if shops refused to send the food to charity.

Now, the last solution I'd like to discuss is rethinking the best-before date, which has been found to confuse shoppers. You see, best-before dates denote the time when a food is at its freshest. In other words, the food in question might still be perfectly edible past that date, but the thing is, people confuse it with the 'use-by date', which shows when an item of food, particularly meat or fish, is no longer considered safe to eat. 

One study suggested that more than 50% of people would bin perfectly edible food if it was past its best-before date. Therefore, many UK supermarkets, including Asda, Sainsbury's, and Marks & Spencer, have scrapped those labels to reduce waste.

I'm sure I could give you much more food for thought, but I've run out of time, so I'll leave it at that. Thank you for your attention.
