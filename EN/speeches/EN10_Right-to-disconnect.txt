Dear colleagues, dear students.

In today's speech, I will be exploring the so-called right to disconnect. If you've never heard of it, you must have been living under a rock these last few years, but don't worry. If you are not familiar with the concept yet, you will be soon enough. I will explain exactly what this right implies and why we might need it.

Okay, so first, let me set the scene. I think you will all agree that we live in a world of unprecedented connectivity. And connectivity seems to have become synonymous with our smartphones, which have become essential extensions of our hands, haven't they? That's because our smartphones boast plenty of advantages.

For example, it has never been easier to talk to friends and family. Smartphones enable communication in all sorts of ways: through calls – which have become a bit old-fashioned – , through text messages, through e-mails, through all sorts of messaging apps (such as Messenger or WhatsApp), and of course through social media. 

Another advantage of our smartphones is our immediate access to information. We now have access to the internet right there at our fingertips. So we can stay updated on current events or we can quickly look things up, anywhere, anytime.

We also have entertainment on the go. If we are bored, albeit for just a few minutes, there's a bunch of fun games to distract us waiting in our pockets. Some of us have been brainwashed into thinking that every free second of our lives must be filled with e-books, games, podcasts, and videos.

And the same is true for work-related content: having constant access to work-related emails, documents, and tools leads us to believe that we have to be productive during and after working hours and we have to be connected all the time. Even when we're away from the office. Even when we're away from school or university. 

Let me give you an example. I'm sure that you can all relate. It's Saturday night and you're out with friends. Despite the marvels of digital communication, it has actually taken you and your friends weeks to agree on a date and a restaurant. You know, complicated work schedules, child-minding duties, food allergies and diets, etc. I think you all know exactly what I mean.

So on that Saturday night, you suddenly get an e-mail from a colleague asking you to send them a document. Now, it doesn't say if it's urgent or not. Maybe they are just working at 10 p.m. on a Saturday because that happens to be convenient for them? Maybe because they spent a lot of time with their kids earlier that week and they're catching up on work? Maybe they don't expect you to respond before Monday?

But the harm has been done, hasn't it? I mean you are a natural people pleaser. You worry that they might need the document as soon as possible. Maybe because they forgot all about an important deadline? Then again, I mean, is their lack of planning your problem? Maybe not, but you want to be a nice coworker. 

You see what's going on here, right? You are torn between ignoring the message until Monday and answering it straight away. And at one point you cave in, thinking: well, I've wasted so much time considering what to do, I might as well just quickly reply. So I can get on with my night out.

I think it's safe to say that this hyperconnectivity is blurring the boundaries between work and our personal lives. Let me give you some figures from the UK to illustrate that. Some 43% of working adults aged between 25 and 34 say they read or send work-related emails outside of office hours. And 38% report making or receiving work calls even while on holiday. 

Now, that is not without risks. Prolonged exposure to work-related communications outside of working hours poses many risks. And I will list just a few. It can strain relationships. It can disrupt sleep patterns. It can compromise overall well-being. It can lead to heightened stress levels, decreased job satisfaction, and even burnout. 

So, in response to these challenges, the "right to disconnect" has emerged. It's supposed to allow employees to unplug from work-related communications. Their bosses or supervisors aren't supposed to bother them during non-working hours. Employees should be able to enjoy their time off without the constant fear of reprisal or negative consequences. 

Several countries have recognized the importance of the right to disconnect and have implemented legislation to that effect. I think France was the first country to implement legislation, but there have been similar movements in Ireland, Luxembourg, Spain, Germany, Italy, and Belgium as well. 

In some cases, companies have taken it upon themselves to introduce a right to disconnect. Volkswagen is perhaps the most notable example. You see, the German car manufacturer actually prevents its email servers from delivering messages to employees outside of their working hours and when they are on holiday.

Now, just in case you're not convinced by the concept just yet. Let me put it this way: Surely, you wouldn't accept your boss banging on your front door on a Friday night to then sit at your dining table demanding urgent answers about work while you are having dinner with your family. So I suppose it makes sense that we shouldn't accept such behaviour digitally either.

Thank you for your attention.
