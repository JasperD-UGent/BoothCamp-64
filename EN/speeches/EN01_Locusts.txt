Dear nature enthusiasts, welcome to another one of our workshops. I'm delighted to see there are so many of you here today. In this presentation, I'll be talking about a fascinating creature: the grasshopper or locust, as most people seem to use those terms interchangeably.

Now, the first thing that comes to mind upon hearing the word 'grasshopper' may well be the famous fable of the ant and the grasshopper. It's about a grasshopper that spent the whole summer singing and dancing, while the ant worked hard to store up food. When the winter arrives, the poor starving grasshopper begs the ant for food, but the ant rebukes the grasshopper's idleness and says it has to accept the consequences of its laziness. But of course, you haven't come here to hear about old fables. You want to find out more about the grasshopper from a biological point of view. 

So, let's start with the basics. Grasshoppers are a group of insects. Specifically, they are among the most ancient living group of chewing herbivorous insects. They came into existence some 250 million years ago. Grasshoppers are typically ground-dwelling insects with powerful hind legs, which allow them to escape from threats by leaping vigorously. It's perhaps interesting to point out that they don't undergo complete metamorphosis. What happens is that they hatch from an egg into something called a nymph or a "hopper", which then undergoes five moults. That is to say, they shed their old skin or rather their exoskeleton, which has grown too tight. With each moult, they become more similar to the adult insect.

Let me tell you a bit more about one particular species of grasshoppers, namely desert locusts. Desert locusts lay eggs in the damp depths of desert soil. A solitary female lays up to 160 eggs in an egg pod. During periods of dry weather, these insects typically live a solitary lifestyle. Adolescent hoppers will spend a few lonely weeks foraging for plants, before reproducing, and dying.

But when a region receives an abundance of rain, the scene is set for a startling transformation. Increased moisture means more vegetation for newly hatched hoppers to eat, which in turn means locusts' numbers increase and they become crowded. The frequent contact amongst them stimulates their leg hairs, triggering the release of a hormone. One that causes them to actively cluster even more. 

These crowds of locusts produce huge amounts of poop, which carries a pheromone that furthers their transformation. The hopper's diet shifts to include plants with toxic alkaloids. Soon, they take on a striking pattern that warns predators of their newly poisonous nature. The adults develop translucent wings and take flight as a full-fledged locust swarm. They reach speeds of 33.79 km/h. And in this gregarious phase, these long-winged, brightly coloured creatures appear so different from their solitary counterparts that they were long thought to be a separate species. 

A typical swarm contains more locusts than there are human beings on the planet. They can reach densities of 80 million individuals per square kilometre. A swarm of that size can consume enormous amounts of food. Specifically, the amount of food equivalent to that eaten by 35,000 people a day.

Now, you might remember that a few years ago, locusts swarmed in large numbers in dozens of countries in Africa and Asia. When swarms affect several countries at once in very large numbers, we talk about a plague. 

You should know that swarming mothers transfer their gregarious condition to their offspring, making it likely that the next generation will form another swarm. That means that while an individual desert locust lives only a few months, a plague can last up to a decade. The potential for a years-long plague isn't unique to desert locusts, isn't unique to that species, but the region they inhabit makes the prospect particularly deadly. You see, their habitat spans some of the world's poorest countries, which are largely populated by people who grow their own food. 

Perhaps an interesting fact: a swarm can cross large bodies of water. In 1988, a swarm even managed to cross the Atlantic Ocean. The locusts likely formed rafts to rest at night, before fuelling up in the morning with a nourishing breakfast of their dead kin. 

Fortunately, a desert locust plague doesn't last forever. When a wet period ends, vegetation becomes scarce and egg-laying conditions decline. As existing swarms die off, new hatchlings spread out in search of food, thus creating enough distance to prevent solitary animals from transforming into gregarious ones. 

Human intervention can also help. Researchers use satellite imagery to identify regions at risk of becoming locust hotspots and they alert local governments. As you probably know, most countries fight back with chemical insecticides, but some regions have found success using fungal diseases that are lethal to locusts but safe for people and the environment. And on that positive note, I'd like to end this presentation. Thank you for your attention.
